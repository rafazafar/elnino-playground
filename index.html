    01 · The machine

    El Niño is what happens when the wind lets go.

    This is a slice of the Pacific cut along the equator: Indonesia on the left, Peru on the right, nearly 18,000 km apart. Heights and depths are stretched so you can see inside.

    ↓ Scroll to run it

    1 · The wind

    Start with the wind.

    The trade winds blow west along the equator all year. They drag the sun-warmed surface water along with them, like breath across a bowl of soup.

    2 · The warm pool

    Warm water piles up in the west.

    Off Indonesia the sea is close to 30°C and stands about half a metre higher than off Peru. All that warmth feeds towering storms — this is where the Pacific's rain falls.

    3 · The cold tongue

    In the east, cold water rises to replace it.

    The dashed line is the thermocline, the boundary between warm surface water and the cold deep. It slopes up toward Peru, so cold, nutrient-rich water wells up there — and feeds the world's largest fishery by volume.

    4 · The loop

    The air closes the loop.

    Air rises over the warm west, flows east high overhead, sinks over the cool east and blows back along the surface as the trade winds. The wind builds the temperature contrast, and the contrast drives the wind. It holds itself in place.

    5 · Letting go

    Now weaken the wind.

    A burst of westerly wind in the far west is enough. The warm water is no longer pinned, and it surges east as a wave beneath the surface, pushing the thermocline down as it goes. The crossing takes about two months.

    6 · The runaway

    Then the loop runs backwards.

    A warmer east means less contrast. Less contrast means weaker winds. Weaker winds let more warm water east. The rain follows the heat out to mid-ocean: Indonesia dries and burns, Peru's desert coast floods, and the upwelling shuts down.

    7 · 2026

    This is the Pacific right now.

    Niño 3.4 is at +3.2°C, the highest weekly reading in NOAA's record. Keep scrolling to December: forecast models carry it to about 4.1°C, a state no instrument has recorded.

    Why two yardsticks?

    The whole tropical ocean is warmer than it was in 1983, so a raw thermometer reading flatters modern events. In February 2026 NOAA switched to a relative index that subtracts that background warming. On it, the record to beat is 1982–83, and this event stood at +1.69°C for July–September.

    What comes after

    Carbon Brief's central estimate puts 2026 at about 1.51°C above pre-industrial levels and 2027 near 1.7°C — comfortably the hottest year ever measured. The UK Met Office calls a record 2027 “very likely.” The previous record, 2024, was about 1.55°C.

    Handle with care

    “Super El Niño” is a media phrase; NOAA says “very strong.” And as Carbon Brief notes, no forecast system has ever been tested against an event this size, because none has ever occurred. In August the models ran about 0.3°C too warm.

    04 · The fallout

    Move the Pacific's rain and you move everyone's weather.

    El Niño doesn't create weather so much as redistribute it — drought here, deluge there, a silent hurricane season somewhere else. Step through the seasons and tap a marker. Solid markers have already happened; ringed ones are what forecasters expect.

    Food prices are already moving

    US export prices, August 2026 vs August 2025 · FEWS NET

    Rice is at its highest since March 2025 on El Niño worries. Global grain output is still forecast to be the second-largest on record — the shock is regional, not global.

    A stronger El Niño does not automatically mean a bigger impact where you live.

    The World Meteorological Organization's caution in its August 2026 update: strength in the ocean does not translate directly into the size of the impact in any one region. Everything on this map is a tilt in the odds, not a promise.
